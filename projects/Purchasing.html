Consumer Spending Power Analysis

Statistical Inference Coursework · R

A rigorous hypothesis-testing workflow on a 2,000-record customer dataset — where the most valuable finding was a null result: spending power turned out to be largely independent of gender, occupation, and income, and we resisted the temptation to claim otherwise.

Problem

Do demographics predict spending power? Retailers routinely segment on gender, occupation, and income. Using a Kaggle customer dataset (n = 2,000, simulated), we tested whether those segments actually differ in spending score — treating it as an exercise in running the full inference toolkit correctly.

Data & Method

  • Descriptive profiling of discrete (gender, occupation, family size, work experience) and continuous (income, age, spending score) variables.
  • Inference pipeline in R: one-sample proportion test; F-test for variance equality before choosing the pooled two-sample t-test; one-way ANOVA with Tukey's HSD post-hoc verification; simple and multiple regression with dummy-coded categorical variables.

Key Findings

p < 2.2e-16

The only strong effect: female share of the customer base significantly exceeds 50%

p = 0.98

No gender difference in spending power (pooled t-test, after F-test confirmed equal variances)

p = 0.454

No occupation effect (ANOVA); all Tukey HSD pairwise intervals include zero

  • Income and age slopes were also non-significant (p = .298 and p = .062) — spending score in this dataset is essentially noise with respect to demographics.
  • The discipline shown: verify test assumptions first (F-test before t-test), confirm ANOVA conclusions with post-hoc intervals, and report nulls as nulls.

Takeaway

In business terms: had this been real data, demographic segmentation would have been a waste of marketing budget — behavioral variables would be the next place to look. Knowing when the data says "no effect" is as important as finding one.
