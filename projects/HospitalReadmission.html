Hospital Readmission Prediction

Healthcare Machine Learning · Group Project · Python, SMOTE, Logistic Regression / XGBoost

Built an end-to-end classification pipeline to flag patients at risk of readmission within 14 days of discharge — a window that matters to both hospitals (bed and care resources) and insurers (Taiwan's standard policy clause treats a 14-day readmission as the same hospitalization). The final logistic regression catches 99.9% of readmitted patients, and the project is equally honest about what the data can't do: with AUC ≈ 0.51, the dataset's signal is too weak for true discrimination, so we framed the model as a first-stage risk screening tool, not a decision-maker.

Problem

Short-term readmissions strain hospital capacity and are a core quality-of-care metric; for insurers, they signal higher claims cost. Both sides share the same pain: high-risk patients are hard to identify before discharge, so follow-up resources get misallocated. The goal: use patient data available at discharge to score each patient's 14-day readmission risk and support triage.

Data & Method

  • Data: 5,000 patient records, 7 features — age, gender, primary diagnosis, number of procedures, days in hospital, comorbidity score, discharge destination. Univariate tests showed no single feature significantly separated readmitted patients, motivating ML models that can capture non-linear and interaction effects.
  • Class imbalance: only 18.8% of patients were readmitted. Compared baseline vs. SMOTE, random over-/under-sampling, and SMOTENC on the training split.
  • Models: Logistic Regression, Random Forest, XGBoost, and SVM, evaluated on Recall, F1, and ROC-AUC (missing a true readmission is the costliest error).
  • Optimization: feature engineering (age groups, stay buckets, high-risk comorbidity flags, age × comorbidity and other interaction terms) plus decision-threshold tuning on a validation split.

Results

  • Best model: Logistic Regression with SMOTE and threshold 0.35 — Recall 0.999, F1 0.654, the highest AUC among the four models. Threshold tuning was the single biggest lever: it moved Recall from ~0.5 to 0.999.
  • Feature signals: age and comorbidity score dominated the coefficients, and engineered terms (age_group, age × comorbidity) entered the top 6 — the model captures age-dependent comorbidity risk rather than a flat linear age effect.
  • The honest read: all four models landed at AUC 0.49–0.52. High recall came at the cost of flagging nearly everyone (Precision ≈ 48.6%). We diagnosed this as weak signal in the dataset itself — not a modeling failure — which is a finding in its own right.

Business Application

We translated the risk score into an insurer-facing early-warning workflow: high-risk patients enter a 14-day post-discharge follow-up list, medium-risk patients get medication and appointment reminders, low-risk patients receive standard digital care. Because insurers already hold claims and hospitalization records, the model slots in as a first-stage screening layer that helps care-management teams decide whom to contact first — assisting triage rather than underwriting, which keeps the deployment barrier low.
