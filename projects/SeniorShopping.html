Online Shopping Behavior of Senior Consumers

Consumer Behavior Research · Survey (N = 139, age 55+) · Factor Analysis, Hierarchical Regression, Moderation

Seniors own smartphones and go online daily, yet few of them shop online — why? We surveyed 139 consumers aged 55+ and found the barrier isn't device skills but psychological safety and perceived security risk. Digital familiarity acts as a buffer: it softens the negative pull of tech anxiety and security fears. Notably, social support (living with younger family) showed no effect — today's senior shoppers rely on their own digital confidence, not their grandchildren.

Background

Taiwan's "orange economy" is projected at NT$3.6 trillion, and E-ICP data shows a striking gap: among the 55–64 group, smartphone ownership is 100% and weekly internet use 99%, but only 25.9% actually shop online. The conversion gap suggested the obstacle sits in trust and risk perception rather than digital access — which is what we set out to test.

Method

  • Survey design: constructs covering psychological factors (tech anxiety, perceived risk), security factors (platform trust, security-risk perception), interface usability, and digital familiarity as a moderator; 155 responses collected, 139 valid after cleaning.
  • Measurement quality: factor analysis confirmed the construct structure; Cronbach's α ranged 0.73–0.92 across scales.
  • Analysis: hierarchical regression in three blocks — demographics (R² = 10.9%), psychological & digital factors (R² = 64.6%), past purchase experience (R² = 71.5%) — plus interaction models for moderation and group-difference tests for the social-support hypotheses.

Key Findings

  • What drives intention (H1 ✓): psychological safety, interface usability, and digital familiarity positively predict purchase intention; security-risk perception significantly suppresses it. Once these enter the model, income — the only significant demographic — loses its effect.
  • Digital familiarity as buffer (H2a ✓, H2b ✓): higher familiarity weakens the negative impact of both psychological insecurity (B = −0.216, p = .002) and security-risk perception (B = −0.198, p = .007).
  • But not for interfaces (H2c ✗): the interface × familiarity interaction was non-significant — friendly interfaces matter for all seniors, not just low-familiarity ones.
  • Experience compounds (H3 ✓): more recent and more positive past purchases strongly predict future intention; experience even displaces platform trust as a predictor.
  • Social support doesn't (H4 ✗): neither household size nor living with younger family affected intention — senior e-commerce behavior has shifted from "assisted" to "autonomous."

Implications

For platforms targeting senior consumers: design age-friendly interfaces for everyone (larger type, simpler flows); make security understandable, not just present, since perceived risk directly suppresses purchase intention; invest in digital-confidence building (guided tutorials, community classes); and lower the first-purchase barrier — because one positive experience is the strongest predictor of the next purchase.
